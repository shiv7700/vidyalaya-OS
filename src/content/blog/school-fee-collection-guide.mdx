In most Indian schools, fee collection is where the office spends the most time and feels the most pressure. Parents arrive in a queue on the last date, cash and cheques need counting, receipts are written by hand, and at the end of the month someone has to work out who still owes what. Good school fee management is less about software and more about a few clear habits. This guide covers the ones that make the biggest difference.

## Start with a clear fee structure

Parents pay on time more often when they know exactly what they owe and when. Before the session starts, write down for each class:

- **Each fee by name** — tuition, annual charges, exam fee, transport, hostel.
- **How often it is billed** — once a year, per term, per quarter or monthly.
- **The due date** for each instalment.
- **The late fee policy**, if you have one.

Keep fee names identical from year to year ("Term 1 Tuition", not "Tution T1" one year and "First term fees" the next). It makes comparisons between sessions easy and avoids charging a student twice for the same thing.

## Bill every student, not just the ones who come to pay

A common habit is to record fees only when a parent pays. That makes it impossible to know who has **not** paid. Instead, create the bill (the amount due) for every student at the start of each term, then record payments against it. The difference between the two is your list of dues, always up to date.

## Give every payment a proper receipt

A receipt protects both the parent and the school. A good fee receipt shows:

- the school's name, address and contact number;
- a **receipt number** that runs in sequence and is never reused;
- the student's name, class and section;
- what the payment is for, the amount in figures **and in words**;
- the payment method — cash, UPI, card, bank transfer or cheque (with cheque number);
- the date, and the name or signature of the person who collected it.

### Never tear up a wrong receipt

Mistakes happen: a wrong amount, a payment against the wrong child. The safe way to correct one is to **cancel (void) the receipt with a written reason** and issue a new one. The voided receipt number stays in the book, marked void. If receipts are simply destroyed or deleted, gaps appear in the sequence and nobody can prove later what happened to that money. Auditors look for exactly this.

## Handle concessions openly

Sibling discounts, staff-ward concessions, scholarships and hardship waivers are part of every school. Problems start when they are given verbally and nobody remembers why a family pays less. For each concession, record:

- the amount waived,
- the reason ("Sibling concession", "Staff ward", "Principal's approval — hardship"),
- who approved it.

This keeps the dues list honest: a waived amount is not a "due", and it should never appear as one in reminders.

## Make it easy for parents to see what they owe

Parents cannot pay what they do not know about. Before each due date, tell them the amount, the due date and how to pay. Many schools send a notice through the school diary, the class WhatsApp group or SMS. It helps even more if parents can check their child's fee statement themselves — what was billed, what was paid, and what is still due — without calling the office.

When sending reminders:

- Send the first one a week before the due date, not after it.
- Be specific: "₹8,200 for Term 2 tuition, due 10 October".
- Keep the tone polite. Most late payments are forgetfulness or cash-flow timing, not refusal.
- Contact families with larger dues personally, and privately — never read out names in class.

## Close every day with a collection report

At the end of each day, the fee counter should be able to answer three questions in a few minutes:

1. How much was collected today, and by which method (cash, UPI, cheque and so on)?
2. Does the cash in hand match the cash receipts?
3. Which receipts were voided today, and why?

Doing this daily catches errors while everyone still remembers the details. Doing it once a month turns small errors into big searches.

## Watch the dues list, not just the collections

Collections tell you how much money came in. The **dues list** tells you where to act. Review it every week, sorted by how overdue each fee is. A short list of families who are more than a month late is far easier to follow up than a long list of everyone who has anything outstanding.
